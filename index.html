Exercise 5.1 — Formation of cations

(1a) Lithium: Li has electron arrangement 2,1. It loses one outer electron and forms Li⁺ with arrangement 2.

Li → Li⁺ + e⁻

(1b) Aluminium: Al has arrangement 2,8,3. It loses three valence electrons and forms Al³⁺ with arrangement 2,8.

Al → Al³⁺ + 3e⁻

(2a) Potassium dot structure: K has one valence electron, shown as K•. After losing it, write [K]⁺ and one released electron: K• → [K]⁺ + e⁻.

(2b) Calcium dot structure: Ca has two valence electrons, shown as •Ca•. It loses both: •Ca• → [Ca]²⁺ + 2e⁻.

Exercise 5.2 — Formation of anions

(1a) Sulphur: S has six valence electrons and gains two to complete its octet. S + 2e⁻ → S²⁻. Electron arrangement changes from 2,8,6 to 2,8,8.

(1b) Chlorine: Cl has seven valence electrons and gains one. Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻. Its arrangement becomes 2,8,8.

(2a) Nitrogen: N has five valence electrons; it gains three to form N³⁻. (2b) Phosphorus: P has five; it gains three to form P³⁻. (2c) Bromine: Br has seven; it gains one to form Br⁻. (2d) Hydrogen: In a simple electron-gain representation it gains one electron to form hydride H⁻ and attain a duplet; hydrogen also commonly forms covalent bonds in molecules.

Exercise 5.2 — (3) Cations compared with anions

  • Cation: formed when an atom loses electrons; it becomes positively charged. Metals commonly form cations.
  • Anion: formed when an atom gains electrons; it becomes negatively charged. Non-metals commonly form anions.
  • In both cases, the atom moves towards a more stable outer-shell arrangement.

Exercise 5.3 — Ionic structures

(a) Mg and O: Mg loses two electrons to become Mg²⁺; O gains those two electrons to become O²⁻. The charges balance in a 1:1 ratio.

Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ O + 2e⁻ → O²⁻ Mg²⁺ + O²⁻ → MgO

Dot-cross drawing: show two electrons leaving Mg (dots) and one entering each of the two vacancies in oxygen's outer shell; put both ions in brackets with 2+ and 2− charges.

(b) Al and Cl: Al loses three electrons to form Al³⁺. Each of three chlorine atoms gains one electron to form three Cl⁻ ions. The ratio is 1:3.

Al → Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ 3Cl + 3e⁻ → 3Cl⁻ Al³⁺ + 3Cl⁻ → AlCl₃

Draw one Al³⁺ and three [Cl]⁻ ions; each chloride has a complete octet, including one transferred electron.

Exercise 5.4 — Recognise ionic compounds

  • KCl: K⁺ and Cl⁻; ionic.
  • AlCl₃: Al³⁺ and 3Cl⁻; ionic in the textbook's electron-transfer model.
  • MgF₂: Mg²⁺ and 2F⁻; ionic.
  • NaF: Na⁺ and F⁻; ionic.
  • NaBr: Na⁺ and Br⁻; ionic.

Recognition rule: a metal transfers electron(s) to a non-metal; write ion charges, then choose the smallest ratio that makes the total charge zero.

Exercise 5.5 — Dot-cross structures

(a) NH₃: Nitrogen has five valence electrons. It shares one pair with each of three H atoms, forming three N–H single bonds, and keeps one lone pair.

.. N / | \ H H H

Each N–H line represents one shared pair. The two dots on N are its lone pair.

(b) HCl: H and Cl share one pair to form H–Cl. Chlorine has three lone pairs remaining; hydrogen completes its duplet and chlorine its octet.

(c) CH₃OH (methanol): Structure is CH₃–O–H. Carbon makes four single bonds (three C–H and one C–O); oxygen makes two single bonds (C–O and O–H) and has two lone pairs.

H | H — C — O — H | .. H ..

The drawing shows the bonding arrangement; place two lone pairs on oxygen.

Exercise 5.6 — Multiple-bond structures

(a) CS₂: Carbon is central and forms a double bond with each sulphur to complete the octets.

S = C = S

Each S has two lone pairs. Carbon has no lone pair in this Lewis structure.

(b) N₂: Each nitrogen has five valence electrons. They share three pairs, forming a triple bond; each N retains one lone pair.

:N ≡ N:

(c) C₂H₄ (ethene): The two carbon atoms share a double bond. Each carbon also forms single bonds with two hydrogen atoms.

H H \ / C = C / \ H H

Each carbon has four shared pairs around it (one double bond and two single bonds). Note: C₂H₄ is ethene, not ethane.

Exercise 5.7 — Bond polarity and coordinate bonds

(1) Polar vs non-polar covalent bond: In a non-polar bond, the shared electron pair is shared equally or nearly equally (e.g. H–H, Cl–Cl). In a polar bond, one atom attracts the shared pair more strongly, producing partial charges (e.g. Hδ+–Clδ−).

(2) Coordinate vs ordinary covalent bond: In an ordinary covalent bond, each atom contributes one electron to the shared pair. In a coordinate covalent bond, both electrons in the shared pair are donated by one atom. Once formed, the pair is attracted by both nuclei.

Useful structures: NH₃ + H⁺ → [NH₄]⁺, with N donating its lone pair (N→H). H₂O + H⁺ → [H₃O]⁺, with O donating its lone pair. NH₃ + BF₃ → H₃N→BF₃, with N donating to electron-deficient B.

Q3–7Long answers · bonding and stabilityFull explanations in straightforward language5 questions

Q3. Importance of noble-gas electronic configuration: Noble gases have complete outer electron shells (helium has two; the other common noble gases have eight). This arrangement is stable, so they are generally unreactive. Other atoms tend to gain, lose or share electrons to reach a similar stable arrangement. This helps predict valency, ion formation and bonding.

Q4. How elements attain stability: Atoms can become more stable by losing electrons (usually metals), gaining electrons (usually non-metals), or sharing electron pairs (covalent bonding). These changes can give atoms an octet or, for hydrogen and a few small atoms, a duplet.

Q5. Ways in which bonds may be formed: (1) Transfer of electrons: one atom loses electrons and another gains them, producing oppositely charged ions held by electrostatic attraction (ionic bond). (2) Sharing of electrons: atoms share one or more electron pairs (covalent bond). (3) Coordinate sharing: one atom supplies both electrons of a shared pair (coordinate covalent bond). (4) In metals, positive metal ions are attracted to delocalised electrons (metallic bond).

Q6. Covalent bond between non-metals: Non-metal atoms share valence electrons so each atom can reach a stable outer-shell arrangement. For example, two chlorine atoms each contribute one electron to a shared pair, forming Cl–Cl. The shared pair is attracted by both nuclei and holds the atoms together.

Q7. Single, double and triple covalent bonds: A single bond contains one shared pair (H–H or Cl–Cl); a double bond contains two shared pairs (O=O); a triple bond contains three shared pairs (N≡N). The number of shared pairs is respectively one, two and three.

Q2–4Core definitions and mole conceptShort and long answersSolved

Q2(i) Mole: The amount of substance containing 6.022×10²³ representative particles (atoms, molecules or ions).

(ii) Empirical vs molecular formula: Empirical formula gives the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms; molecular formula gives the actual number of each type of atom in one molecule. Example: CH₂O and C₆H₁₂O₆.

(iii) Molecules in 9.0 g steam: M(H₂O)=18 g mol⁻¹; n=9/18=0.5 mol; molecules=0.5×6.022×10²³=3.011×10²³.

(iv) Uranium molar masses: U-238 = 238 g mol⁻¹; U-235 = 235 g mol⁻¹ (using their isotope mass numbers as approximate molar masses).

(v) H₂ vs H atoms: A hydrogen molecule contains two H atoms. Therefore one mole of H₂ has twice the mass of one mole of H atoms: about 2.016 g vs 1.008 g.

Q3 terms: Ion—charged atom or group of atoms; molecular ion—charged molecule; formula unit—simplest whole-number ion ratio in an ionic solid; free radical—atom or group with an unpaired electron; atomic number—number of protons; mass number—protons + neutrons; atomic mass unit (u)—one-twelfth the mass of a carbon-12 atom.

Q4 Avogadro's number: One mole of any substance contains 6.022×10²³ representative particles. The particle type depends on the substance: atoms for an element such as He, molecules for H₂O, and formula units for NaCl.

Q3Define allotropes and explain the allotropes of carbon.Long-answer solution · three formsSolved

Definition: Allotropes are different structural forms of the same element that exist in the same physical state. The property is called allotropy.

  1. Diamond: Each carbon atom is covalently bonded to four other carbon atoms in a strong three-dimensional network. This makes diamond extremely hard and gives it a high melting point. It does not conduct electricity because it has no mobile electrons.
  2. Graphite: Each carbon atom is bonded to three other carbon atoms in flat layers of hexagonal rings. The layers can slide over one another, so graphite is soft and slippery. It conducts electricity because it has delocalised electrons.
  3. Fullerenes (for example C₆₀): Carbon atoms form hollow cage-like structures. C₆₀ has a roughly football-shaped arrangement made from pentagonal and hexagonal rings.

Conclusion: These forms contain only carbon, but their different atomic arrangements give them different properties.

Compare in one glance

Diamond: 4 bonds, hard, non-conductor. Graphite: 3 bonds, layers, conductor. C₆₀: hollow cage-like molecule.

Q8Describe the states of matter with examples.Long-answer solution · four statesSolved
  1. Solid: Has a definite shape and volume. Its particles are closely packed and mainly vibrate about fixed positions. Example: ice or iron.
  2. Liquid: Has a definite volume but takes the shape of its container. Its particles are close together but can move past one another. Example: water.
  3. Gas: Has no fixed shape or volume and fills its container. Its particles are far apart and move freely; gases are easily compressed. Example: oxygen.
  4. Plasma: An electrically charged state containing ions and free electrons. It occurs in the Sun, stars and some electrical discharges.

Comparison: Solids keep their shape, liquids flow while keeping volume, gases spread to fill a container, and plasma is an ionised gas-like state.

Q10Examine the concept of solubility.Long-answer solution · definition and factorsSolved

Definition: Solubility is the maximum amount of a solute that dissolves in a specified amount of solvent at a particular temperature (and, for gases, pressure) to form a saturated solution.

Factors affecting solubility:

  1. Nature of solute and solvent: Different substances dissolve to different extents in different solvents.
  2. Temperature: The solubility of many solid substances increases as temperature rises, but this is not true for every substance. For example, the textbook table shows KCl becoming more soluble with temperature, while Ca(OH)₂ becomes less soluble.
  3. Pressure: Pressure has a major effect on the solubility of gases in liquids; increasing pressure generally increases the amount of gas that dissolves.

Related terms: Unsaturated solution can dissolve more solute at that temperature. Saturated solution contains the maximum amount at that temperature. A supersaturated solution contains more dissolved solute than a saturated solution under the same conditions and is unstable.

Worked interpretation

If a table states that 34.7 g KCl dissolves in 100 g water at 20°C, this means 34.7 g is the solubility under those stated conditions—not a universal amount at every temperature.

Q7Importance and uses of isotopesLong answer · examples by fieldSolved

Definition: Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

  1. Medicine: Cobalt-60 is used in radiotherapy; iodine-131 is used in thyroid diagnosis; iodine-123 is used for brain imaging.
  2. Medical tracing: Sodium-24 can help trace blood flow and identify possible blockages.
  3. Research: Carbon-14 can trace carbon movement in biological processes such as photosynthesis.
  4. Dating: Carbon-14 helps estimate the age of once-living materials.
  5. Energy: Uranium-235 is used as fuel in nuclear reactors.

Exam tip: Write the isotope name together with its use so the examples do not get mixed up.

Quick trend compass

Down a group: shielding effect ↑, atomic radius ↑, ionization energy ↓, electron affinity generally ↓, metallic character ↑.

Across a period (left → right): atomic radius ↓, ionization energy generally ↑, electron affinity generally ↑, electronegativity ↑, metallic character ↓. Shielding effect stays nearly constant across a period.

Memory trick

Down = more shells and more shielding. Across = more protons pulling on electrons in the same main shell.

Q2Short-answer questions · 5 partsReady-to-write answers5 answers

(i) Valence configuration of an element in Period 3, Group IIIA: Group IIIA is modern Group 13 and has three valence electrons. In Period 3, its configuration is 3s² 3p¹ (aluminium).

(ii) Define halogens: Halogens are the reactive non-metal elements of Group 17 (VIIA), such as fluorine, chlorine, bromine and iodine.

(iii) Which has higher shielding, Li or Na? Na. Sodium has one more occupied electron shell, so it has more inner electrons shielding its valence electron.

(iv) Why does Na have higher ionization energy than K? Sodium's valence electron is in the third shell, while potassium's is in the fourth. Potassium has greater shielding and a larger atomic radius, so its outer electron is held less strongly and is easier to remove.

(v) Why are alkali metals s-block elements? Their outermost/valence electron occupies an s sub-shell (general valence configuration ns¹).
